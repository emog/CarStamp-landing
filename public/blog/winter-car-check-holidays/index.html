Winter Car Check Before Holiday Driving: Fluids, Tires and Papers

Thanksgiving, Christmas or New Year's: cold mornings, a full car and a long drive to family. Twenty minutes in the driveway now saves a breakdown on a day when every garage is closed.

The short answer

Before a winter trip, check six things: coolant (strength and level), screenwash (a winter mix, never water), engine oil (level, and whether a change is due), brake fluid (its age), tires (tread and pressure, measured cold) and the battery. Then make sure your insurance, registration and inspection stay valid until you're home, and buy any vignette for the countries on your route. Most of this needs no tools, and the checklist at the end fits on one screen.

The fluids, one by one

Coolant (antifreeze)

Coolant keeps the engine from freezing in the cold and from overheating on a long climb. Check the level in the see-through reservoir with the engine cold: it should sit between MIN and MAX. Never open the cap on a hot engine.

The level is only half of it: the mix matters too. A 50/50 mix of coolant and water protects to about -37 °C (-34 °F). A cheap tester from a parts store shows the protection in a minute. If you have been topping up with plain water, the mix may be much weaker than you think. Top up with the coolant type your owner's manual names: types don't always mix. How often to change it depends on the coolant: many modern long-life coolants last about five years, older types about two. The manual gives the interval.

Screenwash

Winter roads throw salt and slush at the windshield, and you use far more screenwash in a week of winter driving than in a month of summer. Fill the reservoir with a winter screenwash rated for the cold where you're going. Plain water freezes in the reservoir and the lines, and can crack the reservoir or burn out the pump. Carry a spare bottle in the trunk.

Engine oil

Check the level on flat ground, as your manual says (usually with the engine off for a few minutes). The number before the W in the oil's grade, such as the 5 in 5W-30, is how well it flows on a cold start: use the grade the manual gives. If an oil change is due in the next month or so, do it before the trip, not after. Old oil protects less on cold starts and on long highway runs.

Brake fluid

Brake fluid takes in water from the air over time, which lowers its boiling point and lets it corrode the brake parts from inside. Many manufacturers ask for a change every two years, whatever the mileage. Check the level in its reservoir (it drops slowly as the pads wear), and look up when it was last changed. If you can't find out, have it tested or changed before a long, loaded trip.

Diesel and AdBlue

Summer diesel can thicken into wax in hard frost and block the fuel filter. In cold months, stations in cold regions sell winter diesel, so if you're driving somewhere colder than home, fill up near your destination rather than carrying a full tank of the local grade. AdBlue (diesel exhaust fluid) freezes at about -11 °C (12 °F). The car has a heater for it, so don't add anything to it, and top it up before you leave, since the car may refuse to restart when it runs out.

Electric cars

Cold weather lowers an EV's range, and a cold battery charges more slowly. Before the trip, warm the car up (precondition) while it is still plugged in, plan charging stops with some margin, and expect busy chargers on the big travel days.

Tires

  • Tread: the legal minimum is 1.6 mm in the UK and most of Europe, and 2/32" in most U.S. states, but grip on snow and slush falls well before that. Many experts suggest 3 to 4 mm (4/32" or more) for winter.
  • Pressure: it drops about 1 psi for every 10 °F (roughly 0.1 bar for every 8 °C) the air cools. Check it cold, before driving, and use the figures on the door jamb label, which often give a higher pressure for a fully loaded car.
  • Winter tires: rules differ by country and region. Germany and Austria require winter tires in winter conditions, Québec requires them from December 1 to March 15, and Bulgaria asks for winter tires or at least 4 mm of tread from November 15 to March 1. Check every country on your route, and carry chains where the signs ask for them.
  • Age: the four digits at the end of the DOT code are the week and year it was made: 4721 is week 47 of 2021. Rubber hardens with age, so old tires grip less in the cold even with good tread.

Battery, wipers and lights

  • Battery: cold weakens a battery just when the engine needs the most power to start. If yours is more than four or five years old, or the engine turns over slowly in the morning, have it tested. Many parts stores test batteries free.
  • Wipers: replace blades that smear or skip. Lift them off the glass on frosty nights, or free them gently before use, never with the wipers switched on.
  • Lights: walk around the car with the lights on, and clean the lenses. Dirty headlights lose much of their light on salty roads.

Papers that lapse over the holidays

Offices close, and renewals that fall between Christmas and New Year's are easy to miss. Check that these stay valid until you're back:

  • Insurance, and if you drive abroad, proof of cover for every country on the route.
  • Registration and road tax.
  • Inspection: the MOT in the UK, the state inspection in many U.S. states, the technical inspection elsewhere in Europe.
  • Vignettes: Austria, Switzerland, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Hungary, Slovenia, Romania and Bulgaria sell road vignettes, most of them online. Buy them before the border.
  • Breakdown cover, and its phone number saved in your phone.

The 20-minute checklist

  1. Coolant between MIN and MAX, engine cold; protection tested.
  2. Winter screenwash full, a spare bottle in the trunk.
  3. Oil level right; no change due in the next month.
  4. Brake fluid level right; changed in the last two years.
  5. Diesel: the tank filled near the destination when it's colder there; AdBlue topped up.
  6. Tires: tread, cold pressure for a loaded car, winter tires where required.
  7. Battery tested if it is four or more years old.
  8. Wipers, lights and the spare or repair kit checked.
  9. Insurance, registration, inspection and vignettes valid for the whole trip.
  10. An ice scraper, a warm layer, water and a charged phone in the car.

Questions about winter car checks

How often should coolant be changed?

It depends on the coolant: many modern long-life coolants last about five years, older types about two. Your owner's manual gives the interval for your car. Between changes, check the level with the engine cold and test the protection before winter.

Can I use water in the screenwash in winter?

No. Water freezes in the reservoir and the lines and can crack the reservoir or damage the pump. Use a winter screenwash rated for the coldest temperature on your route.

How much does tire pressure drop in the cold?

About 1 psi for every 10 °F the air cools, roughly 0.1 bar for every 8 °C. Check the pressure cold, before driving, and use the figures on the door jamb label for a loaded car.

Does diesel freeze?

Diesel doesn't freeze like water, but summer diesel can turn to wax in hard frost and block the fuel filter. Winter diesel, sold in cold regions in the cold months, stays liquid at lower temperatures. AdBlue freezes at about -11 °C (12 °F), and the car has a heater for it.

How old can a car battery be before winter?

Many batteries last four to six years. If yours is four or more years old, or the engine turns over slowly on cold mornings, have it tested before a long trip.
